# Squishy Society — Competitor Analysis

Ten stores in and around the squishy/sensory niche, visited and reviewed in August 2026. Six are Australian and compete with you directly; four are larger international operators worth learning from rather than copying.

---

## The ten sites

**My Sensory Space** (sensoryspace.com.au) — the most direct competitor. Leads hard on value: a scrolling marquee of offers ("UP TO 50% OFF", "SPEND $80 & GET A FREE GIFT"), percentage-off badges on nearly every product, 96 products in the squishy collection alone. Their H1 is engineered for search: "Squishy Toys Australia — 100+ Styles for Sensory Play & Stress Relief", with a subhead explicitly targeting "NeeDoh alternative squishy toys Australia".

**BellaMoon** (bellamoon.com.au) — the opposite end of the same market. Premium, editorial, restrained: cream palette, elegant wordmark, enormous whitespace, full-bleed hero slider. Positions as "handcrafted" and "premium". Most interesting move is their navigation — a category called **Texture**, organising products by how they feel rather than what shape they are. Also runs "Lucky Spoon Mystery Drop" and blind boxes as recurring events.

**Squish Fiddle** (squishfiddle.com.au) — "Your #1 Sensory & Fidget Shop · Sensory Play with a Purpose" on a scrolling marquee. Custom bubble-letter logo with real personality. Navigation reveals the strategy: THERAPY AIDS, BUNDLES, **NDIS ORDERS**. Second announcement bar carries concrete shipping cut-off dates. Hero is an annotated product collage with hand-lettered labels pointing at individual items.

**Sensory Play Store** (sensoryplaystore.au) — two stacked announcement bars (Afterpay, plus a free "Feelings Thermometer Poster" as a lead magnet). Navigation includes **NDIS Orders** and **Schools & Purchase Orders**. Clean white-background catalogue photography. Also runs a blog. A lot of "Sold out" badges were visible, which cuts both ways.

**Adventure Awaits** (adventureawaits.com.au) — the strongest trust-building of the ten. Announcement bar reads "★★★★★ Loved by 200,000+ Aussies · Shipping daily from VIC". Email address *and* phone number sit in the header. Background of the collection banner is a wall of stocked brand logos. Collection page embeds short-form video ("Needoh SOLD OUT?! Try These Squishies") and captures emails with a "NeeDoh VIP List" waitlist. Has a "Things I've Looked At!" recently-viewed feature.

**Aussie Slime Co** (aussieslimeco.com.au) — bold saturated branding (yellow nav, purple accents). Every product photographed in their own branded packaging and jars, which instantly reads as a real business rather than a reseller. Persistent Reviews tab pinned to the screen edge. Collection banner pairs a big headline with a supporting line over product photography.

**Toyworld** (toyworld.com.au) — mainstream retailer, included for contrast. The standout pattern is **circular category tiles** with photography (Dough, Fidget Toys, Putty, Sand, Slime, Squishies, Shop All) sitting above the grid as visual sub-navigation. Filters by Categories, Brands and **Age** — an axis nobody else uses well. Has a product Compare toggle and 84 products with count displayed.

**Squishable** (squishable.com, US) — the most confident brand voice of the ten. Search box placeholder reads "What would you like to snuggle?". Logo carries "EST. 2007 • NYC". Free shipping threshold stated plainly in the top bar ("$59 = FREE U.S. SHIPPING"). Notably, it ships with **accessibility skip-links and an accessibility mode toggle** — rare, and a genuine professionalism marker.

**Creamiicandy** (creamiicandy.com) — the best answer to the problem you actually have. Rather than reselling generic squishies, they built **their own mascot characters** (Yummiibear and friends) and license them out. Their hero banner is a custom illustration that communicates service promises: "We check your items and wrap them carefully", "We include gifts in every order", "Worldwide flat rate shipping". Currency and language selectors. Wholesale and licensing links in the nav.

**Squeezy Store** (squeezy.store) — a cautionary example. Fires an unavoidable modal on landing: free shipping over $49, discount code SQUEEZE5, and a **ten-minute countdown timer**. Escape doesn't dismiss it. The tactics are effective in principle but the execution blocks the site, and countdown urgency is legally risky in Australia — the ACCC has pursued retailers over misleading urgency and false scarcity claims. Worth knowing what not to do.

## What "professional" actually consists of

Stripping out the aesthetics, the same handful of things separate the credible stores from the amateur ones. Almost none of it is visual polish.

**Contactability.** Adventure Awaits puts a phone number and email in the header. Several run live chat. The stores that feel risky are the ones where you cannot find a human. You currently have one email address on a policy page.

**Specific shipping promises.** Squishable states "$59 = FREE U.S. SHIPPING" in the top bar. Squish Fiddle publishes exact cut-off dates. Vague or absent shipping information is the single biggest trust killer in dropshipping, because it is exactly what a scam store also has.

**Visible social proof.** Aussie Slime Co and Squish Fiddle both pin a Reviews tab to the screen edge so it is never more than one click away. Adventure Awaits leads with "200,000+ Aussies". You have no reviews yet — that is fine and normal, but it means you need to lean on other trust signals harder in the meantime.

**Filtering and counts.** Every serious store shows a product count and offers sort plus filter. With 62 products you are now past the point where a flat grid is comfortable.

**A reason to exist.** This is the one that matters most. Squish Fiddle sells "sensory play with a purpose". Sensory Play Store sells to schools and NDIS participants. Creamiicandy sells characters they invented. Aussie Slime Co sells things they make. Every one of them can answer "why buy here instead of AliExpress directly?" A store that is only a nicer-looking product grid cannot.

**Merchandising beyond the grid.** Toyworld's circular category tiles, Squish Fiddle's annotated hero, BellaMoon's texture navigation, Creamiicandy's illustrated service promises. Nobody just lists products.

## Where your site stands

Honest assessment against what I just looked at.

Genuinely fine already: the layout is clean and uncluttered, the pastel palette is on-brand for the niche, category filters work, the cart behaves properly, and the policy pages exist and are specific — which puts you ahead of a surprising number of small stores.

The real gaps, roughly in order of how much they cost you:

**No differentiation.** Right now the site says nothing about why it exists. Every competitor above has an angle — therapeutic, institutional, premium, own-brand, local. You have "Aussie-owned & run" in a trust strip, which is a start but is not a position.

**Trust strip claims things you cannot yet back.** The homepage promises tracked shipping, real human support and 30-day returns. Those need to be true and provable at launch, or removed. Right now there is no contact route visible on the homepage at all.

**Product photography is supplier-grade.** This is the most visible gap. Every credible store either shoots their own product, shoots it in their own packaging, or commissions illustration. Yours are AliExpress listing photos, some with promotional text baked in.

**No social proof anywhere.** No reviews, no ratings, no counts, no testimonials, no user photos.

**The grid is now long.** 62 products with no sub-navigation, no product count, no sort, no search.

**No email capture that works.** The newsletter box is decorative — it isn't connected to anything.

## What I'd do, in order

**First, decide the angle.** Everything else follows from this and it costs nothing but thought. Realistic options given what's already crowded: lean into *curation* (a small, tested, genuinely-picked range versus the 96-product firehose next door), or into *gifting* (ready-to-send bundles, gift notes, occasions), or into *sensory purpose* — though note that NDIS and schools are already well served by two competitors with real credibility there, so that lane is harder than it looks.

**Then fix trust, because it's cheap and high impact.** Put a contact email in the site header or footer where it's visible without hunting. State a specific shipping promise in an announcement bar. Add your ABN to the footer. Either make the trust strip claims true or soften them.

**Then merchandise the grid.** Add category tiles above the products the way Toyworld does — visual entry points beat a filter row. Show the product count. Add a sort control. Consider a "Bestsellers" or "New in" row on the homepage rather than dumping all 62 at once.

**Then photography.** This is the expensive one and can wait until you have stock in hand, but it's what will most separate you visually. Even phone photos of real products on a clean background in consistent lighting will beat supplier images.

**Then social proof.** You can't fake this and shouldn't try. But you can set up review capture from day one so it compounds, and seed it with genuine feedback from your first customers.

**Skip entirely:** countdown timers, fake stock counters, "17 people are viewing this" widgets, and forced entry popups. Squeezy Store does all of this and it makes the site feel less trustworthy, not more — and in Australia, manufactured urgency is the kind of thing the ACCC actually pursues.
